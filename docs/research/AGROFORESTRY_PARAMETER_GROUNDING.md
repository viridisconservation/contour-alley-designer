# Recalibrate the agroforestry designer before Vermont

The Viridis landscape agroforestry designer is only partly grounded in published temperate North American data. Its hydrology ratios, N2O emission factors, root fraction, prairie-strip effects, nitrogen-fixation magnitude and pollination dependence classes match the literature or sit on its conservative side. Its wind-reduction curve is also close. Five defaults that drive results are optimistic or wrongly structured. **Woody carbon growth of 4 tC per hectare of row per year** is about three times the USDA COMET-Planner windbreak value for moist climates (about 1.2 tC/ha, calculated). **The +10% sheltered-alley yield calibration** is a protected-zone figure being used as a whole-field figure; whole-field data from Alberta average −1%. **The 15% edge competition loss** is far milder than measured losses, which run 25–75% near rows on the plains and in humid alleys. **A 60% field runoff cut** was reached by only one prairie-strip layout; the multi-year average is 37%. **Biochar persistence of 0.80** assumes a production temperature that on-farm kilns seldom document. Most economic yields are conservative: chokecherry is 5–8 times below the Manitoba figure, and cider apple prices sit below the cost of production. Several defaults have no citable source at all and should stay labelled as assumptions. These include leakage factors, reversal hazards, the SOC turnover constants, acorn and honey-locust prices, and the cover-crop tissue N value. The most serious problem for the Vermont case is that **no hydrology, shelter-yield or competition data from the Northeast turned up**. Every Vermont default is carried over from the plains, the Midwest or Quebec. Shelter benefit, ET suppression, edge competition and snow loss should therefore become climate-dependent rather than fixed values.

## Most defaults survive, but the high-leverage ones need correcting

The research reviewed about 60 model parameters across six domains. The pattern is consistent. The physical and accounting factors the model borrowed from standard references hold up: the IPCC N2O factors, the Klein pollination classes, and infiltration ratios from Missouri and Iowa. The parameters calibrated to a single optimistic case do not, because they are the ones that generate yield, carbon and runoff benefits. Those cases are a plains shelterbelt at 6.5H spacing, the best prairie-strip layout, and productive alley rows. The table below is the core finding. "Calc" marks a conversion made by the researchers rather than a value stated in the source. "Unverified" marks values the researchers recalled but could not confirm against a fetched source. Where the notes did not state the model's current value, the cell says so.

## The optimistic defaults cluster where benefits are generated

Three results matter most for the model's headline outputs.

The first is **woody carbon**. Temperate hedgerows and windbreaks accumulate about **1.1 tC/ha/yr in aboveground plus belowground biomass per hectare of system** ([Cardinael et al. 2018](https://www.chairedi.fsaa.ulaval.ca/fileadmin/Fichiers/Entente_FAO-UL/Cardinael2018_Revisiting_IPCC_Tier_1_coefficients_for_soil_organic_and_biomass_carbon_storage_in_agroforestry_systems.pdf)). COMET-Planner's moist-climate windbreak coefficient converts to roughly 1.2 tC per hectare of windbreak, biomass and soil combined ([COMET-Planner](https://farmlandinfo.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2022/02/COMET-Planner_Report_Final.pdf)). The model's 4 tC/ha-row/yr is consistent with alley cropping only after COMET's whole-field rate is rescaled to a 20% woody fraction. Per-row conversions are therefore extremely sensitive to the assumed row fraction. A Cardinael silvoarable rate rescaled to rows at 10–20% cover spans 5.7–11 tC/ha-row/yr. The honest fix is to report field-scale carbon next to the published 0.5–1.2 tC/ha/yr range, and to let row growth vary by species and climate.

The second is **yield accounting**. Kort's often-quoted gains (+12% corn, +23% winter wheat) are means over the protected zone of studies that tended to report positive results. Modern yield-monitor data from Kansas and Nebraska find significant gains in only 30–46% of crop-years, and significant losses in 22 of 101 ([Osorio et al. 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10457-018-0270-2)). The best whole-field benchmark is Alberta's 188 site-years, which average **−1% over 0–15H** ([Alberta Agriculture](https://www1.agric.gov.ab.ca/$department/deptdocs.nsf/all/sag3328)). Edge competition is badly understated as well. In semi-arid Kansas, wheat lost about 75% at 0.5H and 25% at 1.6H, and root-pruned rows yielded 1.6 times unpruned ([Lyles et al. 1984](https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/30200525/83-148-J%20Windbreak%20effects%20on%20soil%20water%20and%20wheat%20yield.pdf)). Soybean two metres from five-year-old Quebec poplar yielded 51–63% of the five-metre value ([Rivest et al. 2005](https://giraf.fsaa.ulaval.ca/fileadmin/Fichiers/Publications/rivest_al_2005.pdf)). The researchers proposed keeping 15% for root-pruned plains belts. The Kansas data say even that is mild in dry years, so treat 15% as a floor for root-pruned rows rather than a general default.

The third is **runoff**. The 60% cut comes from one treatment, a 10% footslope prairie strip ([Helmers et al. 2012](https://www.fs.usda.gov/nrs/pubs/jrnl/2012/nrs_2012_helmers_001.pdf)). The robust average is 37% ([Schulte et al. 2017](https://www.fs.usda.gov/nrs/pubs/jrnl/2017/nrs_2017_schulte_001.pdf)), and tree/grass contour buffers on Missouri claypan reached only 1–10% ([Udawatta et al. 2002](https://townofgoshenny.gov/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/Udawatta-et-al_2002.pdf)). Sediment trapping, by contrast, is far more robust than runoff reduction, at 93–98% with prairie strips. The model can claim strong sediment benefits and only moderate runoff benefits.

On the conservative side, the economics often understate tree crops. Vermont orchards yield 15–29 t/ha ([Becot et al. 2018](https://www.uvm.edu/~orchard/fruit/pubs/18Becot_ISHS_CiderAppleMarketVT_v3.2PrePub.pdf)), and mature chokecherry reaches 11–17 t/ha ([Manitoba Agriculture](https://gov.mb.ca/agriculture/crops/crop-management/fruit-crops/chokecherry-production.html)). The model's 10 and 2 t/ha are not neutral defaults either. They understate revenue while the carbon and shelter defaults overstate benefits, so biases in different modules partly offset. A user cannot see that offset in any single output.

## Structural changes the evidence calls for

Several problems cannot be fixed by changing a number; the model's structure has to change.

**Competition and shelter should depend on climate.** Alberta's dry brown soils netted +2–3% while moist black soils netted −3 to −4% ([Alberta Agriculture](https://www1.agric.gov.ab.ca/$department/deptdocs.nsf/all/sag3328)). Shelter saves water mainly when evaporative demand exceeds equilibrium rates ([CSIRO EA02006](https://publish.csiro.au/EA/EA02006)). Shelter gain and et_beta should therefore scale with an aridity index. Edge loss should scale with tree height or age, should be reduced by root pruning, and should widen its decay zone in dry years. The P/PET switch already needed for the IPCC N2O factors can drive all of these, giving one climate classifier for Vermont (wet) and the Flathead and plains (dry).

**Yield should be accounted two ways.** The model should report the protected-zone yield profile (competition at 0–1.5H, peak at 3–5H, zero by 10–15H) and also the whole-field net after subtracting tree land and the competition zone. Osorio's finding that windbreak footprint was compensated in 71% of narrow-belt cases but only 38% of wide-belt cases ([Osorio et al. 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10457-018-0270-2)) shows that belt width belongs in that calculation.

**Snow loss should be applied to snowfall, not to the stored pack.** A monthly loss fraction of 0.2 compounded on the pack removes 60–67% over a winter. That approaches the fallow extreme, not the 15–40% seasonal norm ([Fang & Pomeroy 2009](https://research-groups.usask.ca/hydrology/documents/pubs/papers/fang_pomeroy_2009.pdf)). The trapping reduction should apply only inside 0–15H, because whole-field gains in the Saskatchewan modelling were only 2–3% of peak SWE ([Pomeroy et al. 2011](https://research-groups.usask.ca/hydrology/documents/reports/chrpt11_prairie-shelterbelt-study_apr11.pdf)).

**Planting density, height growth, fixation and pollination dependence should be species attributes.** They should not be global defaults. Nut trees at 9 m spacing and shrubs at 1.2 m differ sevenfold in plants per hectare of row.

**Biochar decay should not be counted twice.** IPCC and Woolf Fperm values already represent total 100-year loss. Layering a 0.1%/yr reversal hazard on top drops retention from 0.80 to about 0.72 (calc). The hazard should cover only physical disturbance and erosion.

**Pollination benefits should phase in from year 3.** Following Blaauw & Isaacs, the benefit should be 0 in years 1–2, apply only to the pollinator-dependent yield fraction, and use separate distance kernels for planted strips and natural habitat.

**Fixation suppression should respond to soil mineral N, not total N applied.** In the red alder trial, slow-release biosolids did not suppress fixation and even raised it, while urea shut it off ([Gaulke et al. 2006](https://revistas.usp.br/sa/en/article/view/22176)).

## Conclusion

The designer's main weakness is not that individual numbers are wrong. Its benefit-generating parameters were each calibrated to the best published case, while its revenue parameters were set at the cautious end. The resulting outputs look balanced but are not traceable to evidence. Moving to climate-switched, species-attributed parameters, reporting carbon and yield at whole-field scale, and labelling the unsourced defaults (leakage, reversal hazard, SOC constants, imputed feed prices) would make results defensible to reviewers and registries. Those changes would also probably lower modelled carbon and shelter benefits for humid sites.

For the Vermont case specifically, the model is currently extrapolating. The highest-value next step is not more literature review of plains windbreaks. It is to find or generate Northeast measurements of near-row competition and tree-row runoff. Until those exist, Vermont results should be presented as sensitivity ranges anchored on Quebec and Ontario alley-cropping data, not on the plains shelterbelt calibration.
